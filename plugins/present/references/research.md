## How to use this evidence

Distinguish measured learning outcomes from design methods and our recommendations. These experiments largely concern students learning technical material. They support hypotheses about comprehension; they do not establish better workplace decisions, persuasion, approval rates, or commercial outcomes. Here, **transfer** means applying taught material to new problems, not transferring a finding to another setting. No source below establishes a universal slide count, word limit, required interaction, or ideal aesthetic.

## Empirical findings

### Garner and Alley: assertion-evidence slides

**Published:** 2013. [How the design of presentation slides affects audience comprehension](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/how-the-design-of-presentation-slides-affects-audience-comprehens/), *International Journal of Engineering Education*, 29(6), 1564–1579.

**Finding:** Among 110 engineering students viewing a technical presentation, the assertion-evidence condition produced better comprehension, fewer misconceptions, lower perceived cognitive load, and stronger delayed recall than conventional slides.

**Apply:** Write a supported sentence headline that states the point. Pair it with evidence that explains the point, such as a comparison, diagram, or annotated example. Check that viewers can connect the evidence to the claim.

**Limit:** The conditions integrated or violated **six multimedia principles**. This was a design bundle, not an isolated test of sentence headlines. It does not prove that changing titles alone improves understanding or that every slide needs a picture. The university record supplies the abstract; avoid adding unreported effect sizes.

### Wolfe, Alley, and Garner: a nonsignificant comparison

**Published:** June 2012, ASEE conference. [High quality visual evidence on presentation slides may offset the negative effects of redundant text and phrase headings](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/high-quality-visual-evidence-on-presentation-slides-may-offset-th-2/).

**Finding:** Two groups, 48 and 52 participants, heard the same recorded MRI explanation with different slides. The assertion-evidence advantage for complex comprehension was **not statistically significant**. Compared with an earlier experiment, conventional slides performed better; these slides included stronger visual evidence and more animation.

**Apply:** Inspect whether the exhibit actually explains the mechanism before enforcing a preferred template. Headline compliance cannot rescue weak evidence.

**Limit:** The suggested roles of animation and visual quality were possible explanations, not separately established causes. A nonsignificant result does not establish equivalence. This study is a counterweight to treating assertion-evidence as a guaranteed improvement.

### Mautone and Mayer: signaling the explanation's structure

**Published:** June 2001. [Signaling as a Cognitive Guide in Multimedia Learning](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.93.2.377), *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 93(2), 377–389.

**Finding:** Three experiments explaining airplane lift found better problem-solving transfer with signals that clarified the material's organization. Signals included preview summaries, headings, and words identifying relationships; they did not add new substantive content.

**Apply:** Make the relevant sequence, cause, or comparison explicit. Use headings and annotations to tell the audience what relationship to inspect. Highlight selectively and consistently; keep the explanation coherent without relying on emphasis alone.

**Limit:** This supports meaningful organizational cues, not arbitrary arrows, color, or bolding. It does not establish that more highlighting produces better memory or that a signaled argument becomes more persuasive. Judge cues by the relationship they clarify.

### Harp and Mayer: irrelevant detail can interfere

**Published:** September 1998. [How Seductive Details Do Their Damage: A Theory of Cognitive Interest in Science Learning](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.90.3.414), *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 90(3), 414–434.

**Finding:** Across four experiments, interesting but irrelevant additions to a science explanation reduced recall of main ideas and problem-solving performance. Emphasis and organizational signals did not reliably repair the damage.

**Apply:** Remove material that competes with the explanation before polishing its layout. Ask what each image, anecdote, or detail helps the audience understand. Preserve qualifications that change the conclusion.

**Limit:** The tested problem was **irrelevance**, not beauty, emotion, or storytelling in general. This is not a ban on relevant imagery, narrative, context, or aesthetic care. Calling necessary background a distraction would misuse the finding.

### Tabbers and de Koeijer: learner control has costs

**Published:** 2010; online 11 November 2009. [Learner control in animated multimedia instructions](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-009-9119-4), *Instructional Science*, 38, 441–453.

**Finding:** In a randomized study of 52 university students, control over a narrated lightning explanation improved transfer but took roughly six minutes versus three and a half. Retention and measured cognitive involvement did not significantly improve.

**Apply:** Break a complex explanation into meaningful stages. Offer pause, replay, or navigation when readers need time to inspect relationships. Budget for that time.

**Limit:** The small instructional study does not separate the benefit of control from additional study time. It does not directly test presenter-controlled progressive reveal. Avoid claiming that interactivity saves time, that animation itself caused learning, or that these results establish workplace benefits.

## Workplace context

### BetterUp Labs and Stanford Social Media Lab: reported workslop

**Date:** Survey conducted September 2025; the [Workslop research page](https://www.betterup.com/workslop) does not state a publication date.

**Finding:** In a survey of 1,150 full-time US desk workers, 40% reported receiving workslop during the previous month.

**Apply:** Check whether a recipient can reconstruct the evidence, context, and requested action without doing the author's unfinished reasoning.

**Limit:** This is commercially published, self-reported survey context, not a causal experiment or population census. It does not prove that AI authorship causes poor communication, or that a particular replacement format fixes it. Do not turn reported experiences into guaranteed time or monetary savings.

## Practitioner precedents

### Ryan Singer: bound the proposal

**Published:** July 2019. [Shape Up: Write the Pitch](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.5-chapter-06); [publisher's July 2019 announcement](https://signalvnoise.com/svn3/shape-up-with-ryan-singer/).

**Method:** Define the problem, available time, solution, likely traps, and exclusions. Rough annotated sketches make the proposed work concrete while leaving room for implementation judgment.

**Apply:** State the recommendation and its boundaries. Include enough of the solution to assess feasibility and expose consequential tradeoffs.

**Limit:** This is a product-development method from practice, not an experiment proving persuasion. Adapt its questions; do not force every communication into its full process or scheduling conventions.

### Bret Victor: make an argument inspectable

**Published:** 10 March 2011; postscript February 2024. [Explorable Explanations](https://worrydream.com/ExplorableExplanations/).

**Method:** Couple an authored argument with a computational model so readers can inspect assumptions and see their consequences. The 2024 postscript distinguishes this ambition from merely adding interactive pictures.

**Apply:** Introduce a control when changing it helps test an assumption or conclusion. Expose the model and sources. Make the initial view meaningful before anyone interacts.

**Limit:** This is a design argument with working examples, not a comparative efficacy study. Inspectability cannot make an invalid model accurate, and interaction is not required for every explanation.

### Amit Patel: comparable views with connected state

**Published:** [Introduction to the A* Algorithm](https://www.redblobgames.com/pathfinding/a-star/introduction.html), 26 May 2014; [Diagram structure](https://www.redblobgames.com/making-of/diagram-structure/), original posts February and March 2018.

**Method:** Red Blob's tutorial introduces inputs, intermediate behavior, and results through progressively richer algorithms. Paired diagrams can share the same map and progress state. The implementation discussion connects controls, inputs, algorithms, outputs, and visualizations.

**Apply:** Hold inputs and visual conventions constant when comparing alternatives. Ensure every dependent result updates when an assumption changes. Introduce complexity in stages that answer successive questions.

**Limit:** These are crafted tutorials and implementation patterns, not controlled evidence of learning or business outcomes. Reuse the explanatory structure without inheriting old library choices. A synchronized comparison still needs honest assumptions and labels.

### Nicky Case: ask for purposeful thought

**Published:** 20 June 2018. [Explorable Explanations: 4 More Design Patterns](https://blog.ncase.me/explorable-explanations-4-more-design-patterns/).

**Method:** Case describes puzzles, prediction before revealing an answer, role play, and sandbox exploration. The examples ask readers to reason about an outcome; clicking by itself is insufficient. Guided steps can prepare readers for a fuller sandbox.

**Apply:** Where useful, invite a prediction, show the consequence, and explain what the mismatch reveals. Use role play to examine a real dilemma. Keep participation optional when it would obstruct quick access to the argument.

**Limit:** These are practitioner patterns, not validated treatments for workplace persuasion. Prediction requires a defensible answer; contested choices need explicit assumptions and competing values. Avoid manufactured suspense or obligatory games.

## Recommendations for this skill

These are authoring judgments informed by the sources, not experimentally proven rules:

- Match the representation to the question: a chart for a quantitative comparison, a diagram for relationships, a concrete example for behavior, or prose when it explains best.
- Preserve the main claim, evidence, uncertainty, tradeoff, and requested action across live, static, and interactive versions.
- Use progressive reveal only when sequence aids understanding. Preserve a complete, readable view for review and export.
- Test comprehension with a person unfamiliar with the work. Ask them to restate the conclusion and its limits; record misunderstandings and revise the explanation.
